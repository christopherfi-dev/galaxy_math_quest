## 2. Step 1: the jars under the sail

### What the child looks at

The sunk raft from above, through clear water. Jars stand in straight rows on its deck. The fallen sail covers the middle. **The whole first row and the whole first column can be seen.** The rest is under the sail.

- The child on the raft says, before anything is asked: "My uncle packs every row full. Every row is the same."
- Nothing on the picture is a number.
- **A finished example, about another raft:** the child's own small raft is beside the pier, with nothing over it: 2 rows of 3 jars. "My raft has 2 rows of 3. That is 6 jars."

### What goes in the notebook

The child's own sketch: the same picture, small. It is shown again at the float maker's, so the question there is about maths, not memory. [The same fix a reviewer asked for at the well.]

### What could go wrong here

- A child counts only the jars that show (the row and the column). The words "every row is full, every row is the same" are the guard. A nudge says them again.
- Rows and columns swapped: it does not matter. The child gives one number, the jars.

## 3. Step 2: the float maker's problem

- **First:** "How many jars are on the raft?" A wrong number: "That does not sound like my brother's packing. Look at your sketch again. Every row is full." No number is given.
- **Then, in her words:** "One float holds up 2 jars. I tie 4 floats on a string. How many strings must I make?"
- **The picture:** her workshop. On the wall: one string of 4 floats. Under one float hang 2 jars. Nothing shows what a whole string holds, and nothing shows the answer.
- **Two ways to think, both right:** a string holds 8 jars, and 24 jars need 3 strings. Or 24 jars need 12 floats, and 12 floats make 3 strings. The game asks only for the strings.
- **A wrong number:** she ties that many strings in her head. "That would not hold them up exactly." No direction. A nudge: "How many jars can one string hold up?"
- **The finished example,** told before she asks, about a boat and sacks: "Last year I lifted a boat with 18 sacks in it. A float held up 3 sacks. I tied 2 floats on a string. So a string held up 6 sacks. Six, twelve, eighteen: 3 strings."

## 4. Step 3: picking pods

- **In her words:** "A float is blown from one marsh pod. I need one pod for every float. No more: a pod left over goes bad by morning. Pods hang in bunches. Pick a bunch whole, or it tears."
- **She does not say how many pods.** The child works it out: strings × floats on a string.
- **The picture is the pod bank:** twelve bunches hang on the plants. Each bunch is drawn with its pods: bunches of three sizes (say 2, 3 and 5). No numbers.
- **The child taps a bunch to pick it.** It moves to the basket, drawn as it is. Tap it in the basket to put it back. **Nothing adds the basket up.**
- **More than one basket is right.** For 12 pods: 5 + 5 + 2. Or 3 + 3 + 3 + 3. Or 2 + 2 + 3 + 5.
- **Nobody at the bank checks.** The float maker counts when the child comes back. A wrong count: "I need a pod for every float, and no more. This is not right." The child keeps the basket, walks back and changes it. [This costs a walk of about 20 steps. It is the world answering, as your offline tasks did. See decision 3.]

## 5. Step 6: the Makers' scale

- **The picture:** a scale with two pans, stuck level by a pin. On the left pan: 3 sealed jars, all the same, and a weight marked 4. On the right pan: one weight marked 19. A dial under the jars reads "one jar = ?".
- **The child sets the dial and pulls the pin.**
  - Right: the pans stay level. The scale opens.
  - Wrong: the scale tips to one side, as a real scale would. The pin goes back in.
- **This shows which way the guess is wrong.** A real scale does. A child may guess, look and guess again. That is a real way to solve it at Grade 3. The report counts the tries.
- **The finished example,** scratched on the scale's foot, about boxes: "2 boxes and a 3 weigh the same as 11. Take the 3 away from both sides: 2 boxes weigh 8. One box weighs 4."
- **It is the Makers' box turned round.** The box gave the number in and asked for the rule. The scale gives the rule and asks for the number in. The note inside says so: "Every rule can be run backwards."

## 8. What could prove this wrong

- **Too like the well.** Both are "measure it, tell a maker, fetch a material, come back". That is your lantern shape. But two in one region may feel the same. The maths differs: the well is one step at a time; this is two steps, a choice of bunches, and a rule run backwards.
- **The hidden jars.** If children count only what shows, the words did not work.
- **The walk back from the float maker** with a wrong basket may feel like a punishment.
- **The scale can be solved by guessing.** At most seven guesses.
- **Words:** float, string, pod, bunch, sealed, scale, pan, pin, dial.
- **Nobody has played anything like it.**

## 10. The review: 2 blockers, 15 major. Not built.

The reviewer worked every number again, and listed every case the number rule allows (there are only 34).

### The blockers

| | Blocker | A case |
|---|---|---|
| B1 | **The scale cannot work as I wrote it.** Sealed jars weigh what they weigh. Turning a dial does not change them, so a real scale would not move. And the tip reads two ways | The child sets 6 and the left pan drops. "My number is too big"? Or "the jars are heavier than I said, go higher"? |
| B2 | **"Every rule can be run backwards" is false.** "Times 0" cannot be | |

### The major faults, in short

- **It is the well again,** beat for beat: measure by multiplying, tell a maker a number, the maker's sum, fetch bundled plants, come back, raise a thing out of water, a Makers' device with "× a then + b", an epic card. A child will feel it.
- **The scale needs no maths.** It works out 3 × the guess + 4 for the child and compares. Three turns of the dial open it.
- **The scale is one equation,** and its foot gives the whole method.
- **The strings problem lets wrong methods pass.** In 17 of the 34 cases the answer is the number of rows, or of jars in a row: it can be read off the sketch. In 5 more, adding the two numbers gives it. Only 12 cases are clean.
- **Little spread:** 44% of the cases have 24 jars. A row of 7 never survives.
- **"That would not hold them up exactly"** will be read as "too few". And 5 strings do hold up 24 jars: the world refuses a true answer, with no story reason for "exactly".
- **A child who answers with the floats** has done the first step right, and is told flatly "wrong".
- **The finished example is the same problem** with sacks for jars.
- **A wrong basket** can mean a wrong target or a wrong sum. "This is not right" says neither, and costs two walks.
- **One pod too many is refused** for a made-up reason. "Then throw it away," a child will say.
- **"The sail covers the middle"** and "the first row and first column show" are two different pictures.
- **The small raft, all its jars showing,** models counting by ones: the very error I feared.
- **The typed world is ignored.** A child will type "pull sail", "dive", "take jar". Taking jars off is the plain cure for a raft packed too full. The note gives no reason these fail.
- **Decisions made silently:** every number range, the epic card against your earlier rule of a random card [Conflict: 16 November and 22 November], whether the well comes first, and more.

### What I take from it [Proposal]

1. **A second lantern quest in the same region should not have the same bones.** Your lantern shape is yours. But two of them, in the same three towns, both ending in a Makers' device, read as one quest done twice.
2. **The good parts are worth keeping for somewhere else:** jars in rows with part hidden; a basket that can be filled more than one right way.
3. **The sunk raft stays a "not yet" place.** It still says why.

### Two things I need from you

1. **Is one mini quest for each region enough for a first test with children?** Or do you want two, and of what kind: another lantern, or something of another shape (a trade, a race against the tide, a mystery with clues)?
2. **Does a mini quest give an epic card every time** (your words of 22 November), **or a random card** (16 November)?
