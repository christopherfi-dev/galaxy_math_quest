## 0. In short

- **Your maths needs a year.** "How much by month 11?" only means something if a people must decide early, and wait.
- **Your own page gives the people their way of life.** "Multiplica and Dividia... a delicate balance between multiplication and division." [Source] On Algora those are the two suns, and the two halves of the year: grow, then share.
- **One cause:** the Makers' machines did all the planning. The Gate broke. The machines went dark. Nobody remembers how to plan a year by hand.
- **The table the big world thread asked for is section 1.** The rest explains it.
- **Three things nobody had decided** have options in section 6.
- **Twenty-eight decisions are yours.** Section 9. That is too many. The maths thread's note names the five that matter for a first build, and I agree with its list. Three shape the rest: why the Gate broke, the year, and "decided once".

### The story in one breath [Proposal]

1. The Algorans live under two suns.
2. Under the gold sun everything comes in by the same amount each month, and under the white sun the high water comes and they live on what they stored.
3. So the big things are decided once, early: what to sow, what to promise, whom to hire.
4. For sixty years the Makers' machines made those decisions, and now the Gate is broken and the machines are dark.
5. The cadet decides for a town by counting ahead, the year shows how it went, and the town's keeper learns to do it herself.

## 2. Who the Algorans are

All [Proposal] unless tagged.

- **Everything comes a little at a time.** A plot is picked once a month. A boat comes home once a month. Under two suns, things grow fast and steadily. Nothing can be hurried. So a town must decide in Gold month 1 for what it will need in White month 3.
- **Two suns** [Built]. The gold sun is **Multiplica**. The white sun is **Dividia**. [Source: your names.]
  - Under Multiplica, things grow. That is the maths you want now.
  - Under Dividia, a store is shared out, month by month and house by house. That is division. It waits there for you.
  - Their saying: "Grow by the gold sun. Share by the white."
- **Every store is empty at New Year.** What is left on the last day of White month 3 is eaten at the New Year feast. So every count starts from nothing. That is your "3, 6, 9, 12", for a reason a child can be told.
  - **Later:** a town may choose to keep some back. Then a count starts with some already. That is your "3x + 2".
- **In the gold months they eat as they go.** The river feeds them. The store is only for the white months.
- **Without counting ahead, a town does too much of everything, to be safe.** It sows every scrap of mud. It hires carts all year. It works without rest, and still its evenings are dark. Section 3.
- **The big things are decided once.** Seed takes only in sowing week. Keepers trade only at the Lake Market. Every cart-month is promised at New Year. After that, a town can only watch the pile climb. **This is why counting ahead matters here,** and why a town cannot simply wait and see.
- **A town has only so much.** So many plots. So many reeds. So much food to pay with. More of one thing is less of another, in the same town and the same picture. Section 6c.

- **What was lost is exact.** A keeper can count what has come in. She can say what the town needs: she has every old row in front of her. What nobody can do is work out, in the first week, what will reach that line. That is why a table is kept (the cuts), and why a child's plan is worth something.
- **The Makers were these peoples' own great-grandparents.** (Decision 6. It changes no built words: Elder Rivet says only "long ago".)
- **The keeper could do it, if someone showed her.** She can count. She has pips. Nobody has laid them out ahead in sixty years. The first year the cadet does it, and she watches. That is the arc: the cadet does not replace the keepers. The cadet gives the skill back.
- **What a child should come away knowing:** a people handed their planning to machines, and forgot how. Planning ahead is what keeps a town fed.

**The Meadow's machines [Built] have no date.** They can be done in any gold month.

### How a deciding year goes

1. **At New Year one town asks for help,** with one decision.
2. **The cadet decides.** For A and C, that is in the first week. For B, the year runs to the Lake Market first, and the cadet reads four cuts on the wall.
3. **The cadet says: "Let the year run."** In that town's picture the months go by, one count day at a time. The pile climbs by the same amount each time. A child watches the count happen.
4. **The white months show how it went,** in that same town.
5. **New Year again.** Another town asks, or the same one. The numbers are new, because the Twinseers have new words: a stronger sun, higher water. This is "a new problem of the same size". A harder year arrives without a word.
6. **Nothing is carried over.** Every year is fresh. A thin White month 3 ends at New Year.

### What the keeper does about the other two

**So that only the cadet's decision can cause a miss,** a keeper who has not learned a thing does it the safe way. Safe means too much: more work, more pay. Only one of her safe ways leaves the town short, and that one is asked first.

| | Until she has learned it, the keeper... | What that costs | Is it seen as a miss? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1, the mats and the oil** | promises only a few mats, to be safe | too little lamp oil. The lamps go out early in the white months. | **Yes. It is the one trouble every town has, the year after the machines went dark. So it is asked first.** |
| **2, sowing** | sows every scrap of mud, the poor far plots too, half food and half reed | enough of both, and a great deal of wasted work | No. The town is fed and dry. It is tired. |
| **3, the carts** | hires the carts for all nine months | fish, to pay for months she did not need | No. She keeps enough fish back. The wall is always finished. |

- **A year of the first kind:** the far plots are sown and the carts come all year. If the cadet's word at the Market is right, the lamps burn all evening again.
- **A year of the second kind** comes after the first is learned. The keeper says: "This year we sow only the good near plots. There are just enough. How many for food?" The lamps are lit. The carts come all year.
- **A year of the third kind** comes after the second is learned.
- **When a keeper learns:** after a year that came out right. How many right years it takes is the engine's choice, not the story's. Until then she asks the same kind again, with new numbers.
- **When she has learned it,** she does it herself and it comes out right. She asks for the next kind, or the same kind grown harder: "We are more houses this year."
- **A town the cadet has never helped** has dark evenings, tired people and fewer fish than it could have. It is never hungry and never flooded.
- **The Furrows** sell the oil and own the carts. Their first kind is about their own pots of oil, promised for mats. They have no third kind.

**What this says about "too much":** a town that cannot count ahead does too much of everything, to be safe. That is not wicked. It is tiring and costly. Counting ahead is what lets a town do just enough, and have its evenings back.

### What lasts, and what does not

- **A miss does not last.** A dark evening, a thin month, a wet floor: each ends at New Year.
- **What the cadet gives a town does last.** A town that has learned the first kind has lit windows every white month from then on. After the second, its far plots lie fallow and flower, and people sit in the evening. After the third, fewer carts come down the hill road and the fish racks are fuller. **A child can look across the Reach and see which towns they have helped.**
- **And a mark on the store wall,** in the child's colour, for each year a plan was made.

### Where exploring comes in

Between years. The town that asks is somewhere else on the map. The old walls, the songs and the coin are found on the way. A year is one decision. The walk to it is the world.

**A step down is a family.** When the engine steps a child down, the next New Year a family asks, not a town. Same idea, a few plots.

**This is the story's need, not a design.** How it is drawn and coded is the big world thread's. Three things are theirs to solve, not mine:
- In a world a whole class shares, whose year is it?
- How long "the year runs" takes on screen.
- That a miss always shows. If a town is over on one thing and under on another by amounts that cancel, the picture might show nothing.

- **Each people has a custom of its own** (the table in section 1), so they are not one people in five costumes. This is a start, not enough. Homes, food and play are still to write.

## 5. The three decisions, in full

Your words: "they must analyze it and make a decision." [Source]

**Before any decision, a finished example:** an old row on the store wall, from another year and another thing. A line, then cuts climbing to it by the same amount.

**What is the same every time**
- "Each" always means each one: each plot, each weaver, each cart.
- A day is always named the same way: "on count day 7, after the count".
- The cuts on the wall are the whole town's pile. So the keeper can say what **all** the town's weavers, or plots, bring in on one count day. Then a row is plain multiples. "Each weaver makes so many, and we have so many weavers" is the same row, a size harder. Which one a child is given is the maths thread's to choose.
- Nobody says "wrong". The year runs, and the picture shows what followed.
- A miss ends at New Year.

**The lines the keeper speaks below are examples of the kind of fact she gives.** The numbers are the maths thread's.

### 1. How much? [Source: "How much do they need by month 11"; "if this growing pattern continues", 22 November, in `INFO_FROM_MARC.md`]

This kind is fitted by the maths thread (`proposals/GMQ_Big_World_Maths_Fit_v1_2026-11-23.md`, section 2). The story below is written to match it.

- **When:** the Lake Market, after count day 4. Only keepers may trade, and this is the one time they leave their towns.
- **Why mats:** the weavers make mats from wild river reed, the same number every count day. Every mat is for trade. None is kept.
- **Why lamp oil:** the Furrows press it from hill nuts, and bring it to the Market. No river town can grow it. Without it the white months are dark by supper.
- **The tally stick.** The store wall stays in the town. So a keeper never leaves home without her stick: this year's row, cut small. At the Market it has four cuts. Beside each is the size of the pile that day.
- **The keeper does not say how much comes each count day.** She has never thought of the pile that way. She hands over the stick.
- **The oil seller says:** "My raft comes on count day 7, after the count. Every mat waits at home till then. How many mats will there be, in all?" (The count day is hers to name. It is a different one each year.)
- **The cadet's number is the deal.** A word at the lake is kept.

**What follows, in the story's words**
- **At the Market:** "One pot for each mat you promise." She pours them. And she ties one hook on her raft for each mat. That hook is the town's now. She cannot give its place to another town.
- **Said right:** every hook gets a mat. The lamps burn all evening, to New Year.
- **Said too few:** every hook gets a mat, and mats are left on the dock with no hook. They can be counted. The town has that many pots fewer than it could have had.
- **Said too many:** hooks sail empty. They can be counted. "One pot back for the mat I did not get. One pot back for the hook that sailed empty." Two pots for each empty hook.
- **Said far too many:** she takes back every pot there is. She cannot take a pot that is not there.
- **Why two:** an empty hook cost her twice. She poured oil for nothing, and she carried a place she could have sold.
- **So too few and too many cost the same.** Neither "always say less" nor "always say more" is safe.
- **The road from the number to what follows:** the dock, the oil shelf, the lamps.

**A finished example first:** on an old stone at the Market, another keeper's stick from long ago, and what she said, and that the raft took every mat. (The maths thread's section 2 has it.)

### The small row: a child's own rack

When a child is stepped down, the row must be small in the story as well as in the numbers. And it cannot be a trade: only keepers trade.

- **A child of the town has a chore.** One thing each count day, in the bundle it always comes in:
  - **a pair** of reed sandals, plaited for the family's feet in the wet;
  - **a hand of five** plums, strung to dry;
  - **a bundle of ten** fire sticks, tied for the hearth.
- **The child's gran ties the rack in the first week,** while the reed is green: one loop for each thing the child says there will be, on the count day the rain is due.
- **Too few loops:** on that day, things with no loop are left on the floor, and spoil in the wet.
- **Too many loops:** empty loops. The green reed they used was for patching the roof. One patch is not done, and drips.
- **It is small:** one child, one chore, one bundle a count day. A bundle of ten sticks is less than one mat.
- **How the numbers of the two sides compare** is the maths thread's to set.

### The old singer: the one who teaches

Nobody on Algora can count ahead. So nobody can teach it. But one thing was never handed to the machines: a song.

- **An old Rillfolk singer** [Built: the Rillfolk sing] knows a counting song from her gran. Children skip to it.
- **It has three verses:** the sandals, by twos. The plums, by fives. The sticks, by tens. Each line is one count day more.
- **She does not know what it is for.** To her it is a skipping song. It always stops at today.
- **What the cadet finds:** sing one line past today, and the song says what will be. That is counting ahead. It was in the song all along.
- **How she teaches:** she sings a line and stops. The child gives the number. If the number does not fit the tune, she lays out pips and sings the line again. She is teaching a song, and a singer may say "again".
- **The towns' rows have no verse:** threes, fours, sixes, sevens. The cadet makes the new verses. That is the skill moving from the small row to the town's.
- **The keeper watches, and learns the new verse.** That is what "she has learned it" means.

### 2. How many? [Source: "How many farms do we need"]

- **When:** sowing week. Seed takes only in the new mud. A plot not sown this week grows nothing this year.
- **The keeper says:** "This year we sow only the good near plots. There are just enough." What the town needs in its store by Last Raft. What a plot of food brings in each count day this year.
- **The cadet decides:** how many plots are sown with food. The rest are sown with roof reed. (Wild river reed is cut for mats and rope [Built: the reed cutter]. It is too short for a roof.)
- **Too few food plots:** the pile stops below the line. White month 3 is thin. Bowls are smaller. Then New Year.
- **Too many food plots:** more food than the town needed. But the reed pile is short, in the same picture. Some roofs are not mended before the rain, and drip.
- **The road:** the number, the two piles, the bowls or the roofs. Two steps.
- **The small version:** a family's garden of a few plots. Food, or reed for their own roof.

### 3. For how long? [Source: "how much stone do we need in the 12th month", 22 November, in `INFO_FROM_MARC.md`]

- **When:** New Year. The Furrows come down to shake hands. They have carts enough for what is agreed that day, and no more.
- **Why a wall every year:** a low wall of river stone breaks the first rush of the water. The water rolls its stones down the bank. A wall that could stand for ever would take mountain stone, and more carts than there are.
- **The keeper says:** how many blocks this year's wall needs. (The Twinseers have said how high the water will come.) What the carts bring on a count day.
- **The cadet decides:** for how many months to hire the carts. Each month is paid for in fish, at Last Raft.
- **Too few months:** the carts stop coming. The wall is short at one end. In White month 1 the water comes over there. Wet floors. The town bails out together.
- **Too many months:** the wall is finished, and the carts still come. A deal is a deal. The fish that paid for them is missed: the jars run out in White month 3. The spare blocks lie in a heap. The wall is no better for them.
- **The road:** the number, the wall or the fish jars. One step each way.
- **The small version:** a family's doorstep wall, and a neighbour's hand cart for so many months, paid in jars of fruit.

**The decision already built** is a fourth: **send.** The Reach's fleet [Built] is the Last Raft job.
