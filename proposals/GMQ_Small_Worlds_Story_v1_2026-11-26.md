# The 20 small worlds: a reason for the maths, in one kit rule

**Date:** 26 November 2026. **For:** Marc, and the main thread. **From:** the story thread.
**Stage:** a design note. Nothing here is built. I changed no file of the main thread's.
**Reviewed:** see section 8.

**Tags:** [Built] is in the game today (version 100). [Source] is your words or pages. [Proposal] is mine.

**Numbers:** none new. Each job below uses the number ranges the game's lock already uses, which the main thread made and checked.

---

## 0. In short

- **The trouble** (the main thread's check, which I confirmed): every small world hides a star seed in "a shell box" with "a number lock". The lock asks for the nearest ten or hundred, a sum, or a difference. Nobody needs the answer. A wrong one changes nothing. The seed is only counted.
- **Your rule** [Source: Marc, 25 November]: a code or a rule is never a thing in the story. The maths is how the world works, so that something can happen.
- **The fix, for all 20 at once** [Proposal]:
  - The shell box and its lock go.
  - Each world's creature has a small trouble that needs that world's maths. It cannot count, so it shows you.
  - When you help, its plant drops a star seed. The seed is for Project Second Home: a seed that can grow far from home.
- **Three scenes cover the four kinds of question** (section 3):
  - **Where to stop** (nearest ten or hundred).
  - **Fill the pod** (the difference).
  - **Balance the scale** (the sum).
- **A wrong answer plays out, and shows why** (your rule for after the station). Then a new problem of the same size.
- **Your decisions are in section 6.** Three.

## 2. The story each world tells [Proposal]

**Told on landing, in one added line.** The landing already says: "You land on Ello, Planet A. The sky is deep purple and orange. Sharp peaks stand far off. A creature watches you." Add: "**This is the world you traded with. Its trader is the creature.**"

- **The creature is its world's trader and gardener.** It gathers its world's goods (glow moss, star salt, sky honey...) and leaves them at the post for passing ships. It looks after the plant that grows its world's seeds. It cannot count. It does not use words.
- **Why it needs the cadet:** its trouble is one count. It shows the trouble, and waits. Watching it is how a child finds out what is wrong. That is your Novus way: describe, do not label, and discovery comes from doing.
- **What a star seed is:** a seed of this world's own plant, one that can grow far from home. Project Second Home asks every cadet for them, because a new home will need plants. The cadet keeps each seed in the ship's seed tray, and its log row goes home with the rest.
- **One line when the first seed is found:** "Earth will want this. A seed that grows far from home is worth more than gems."
- **What this adds that was missing:** a child arrives, and knows what this place is (the world they traded with), who is here (its trader), and why the seed matters (Earth's new home). That is your rule 5.

## 3. The three scenes

**The same in all three:**
- Every line a child reads is one card of 120 characters or fewer.
- The scene fills the picture.
- The creature shows the trouble; the line under the picture says it in a sentence.
- The child types a number, as now.
- Nobody says "wrong".
- After a wrong answer the scene plays out, the creature tries again next time, and the numbers are new.

**How a scene starts:** as now. Talk to the creature twice; it looks at its plant; touch the plant. The plant no longer hides a box. It shows the trouble.

### 3a. Where to stop (nearest ten; nearest hundred)

- **What the child sees:** the ground from the side, with the creature's hop marks every ten paces (or float marks every hundred). The creature is at one end. Its seed plant stands at, for example, 47 paces.
- **The trouble, in two short lines:** "The [creature] moves ten paces at a time. Its [plant] opens for only a little while each evening." / "The grass is taller than the [creature]. It cannot see the [plant]. You can. Which mark should it stop on?"
- **Why it needs the nearest:** it walks the rest of the way on its own small legs, or rolls, if it has none. The nearest mark is the shortest walk.
- **Right:** it stops on the nearest mark, walks 3 paces, and the plant opens for it. The plant drops a seed at your feet.
- **Wrong, too far or too near:** it stops on another mark and walks 7 or 13 paces. The steps are drawn and counted as it goes. **It gets there after the plant has closed:** the fan leaf "folds shut", the lamp fruits go dark, the puff ball sinks. It sits down. Next evening it starts from somewhere else, so the walk is new.
- **The finished example, the first time a child meets this scene:** the creature makes one easy trip on its own, to another of its plants, which stands exactly on a mark ("40 paces. It stops on 40. No walking at all."). Its seed plant is not on a mark. That is when it needs you.
- **For the hundreds:** "It floats a hundred paces at a time." The marks are hundreds, and the walk is drawn in tens.

### 3b. Fill the pod (the difference)

- **What the child sees:** a seed pod hanging from the creature's plant, with marks up its side. A **fill line** shows what the pod needs before night. The level inside shows what it holds now.
- **The trouble:** "The pod needs [400]. It holds [130]. The [creature] will fetch more from its heap. How many more?"
- **Why exact:** a pod seals only when it is full to its line. Over the line, it cannot close over the heap.
- **Right:** the creature fetches that many. The pod fills to the line and seals. The plant drops a seed.
- **Too few:** a gap shows between the level and the line, counted. The pod cannot seal. **Too many:** the rest spills over onto the ground, counted. The pod cannot seal. Both are seen in the same picture, and each costs the same: no seal tonight.
- **The finished example:** the first time, the creature fills a small pod on its own, by counting up from what it holds to the line in tens. The child watches it count.
- **The game's own hint already fits:** "Count up from [b] to [a]. Or take [b] away from [a]."

### 3c. Balance the scale (the sum)

- **What the child sees:** the trade post's scale. The creature has put two bags of its goods on one pan. The other pan is empty, with weights beside it.
- **The trouble:** "A ship will take the goods only when the scale is level. One bag weighs [230] grams. One weighs [340] grams. What weight should go on the other pan?"
- **Why it matters:** the post sells by weight. A level scale is a fair trade.
- **Right:** the scale is level. The ship takes the bags and leaves gems in the post's tray. The creature does a happy wiggle, digs under its plant, and gives you a seed.
- **Too light, or too heavy:** the scale tips one way or the other, and the line under the picture says which side is heavier. No ship will trade on a scale that tips. Next time, new bags.
- **The finished example:** the first time, the creature levels a small scale on its own, with two light bags and an easy sum.
- **Grams** are allowed [Source: Marc, 23 November: "Grams is fine."]. The game's hint already fits: "Add the hundreds. Then add the tens."

## 5. Pushing like a child

| A child asks | The story answers |
|---|---|
| "Why can't the creature count?" | It is a trader and a gardener, not a counter. It does not use words, and it has never needed numbers until a ship's scale did |
| "Why doesn't it just hop to the plant?" | It cannot see it over the grass. You can |
| "Why can't the pod just be a bit over?" | It cannot close over the heap. That is how this plant is |
| "Why not just guess the weight?" | The ship will not trade on a tipping scale |
| "Why do I want the seed?" | Earth is looking for a new home. A new home will need plants that grow far from Earth |
| "Can I take the plant?" | As now: "It will not let go of the ground", "It is far too big to carry" |
| "What happens if I get it wrong?" | You see what happens: the long walk, the gap, the spill, the tipping scale. Then the creature tries again, with something new |
