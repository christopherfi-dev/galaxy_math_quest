# What independent evidence says about digital tools and maths learning at about age 8 (Grade 3)

Research notes, compiled 2026-11-17. These notes supply an evidence base only. They do not compare the two products.

**How to read these notes**

- Every finding has a link. "Inferences" are my own reasoning, not sourced facts.
- Effect sizes are in standard deviations (SD, d or g). As a rough guide from the sources below: good maths practice software usually lands at 0.05 to 0.20 SD; good human tutoring at about 0.3 SD.
- "Older students" is flagged wherever a study is not about ages 6 to 10.
- Many numbers came from search summaries or secondary pages, not the full paper. Those are flagged "secondary".
- **Could not open:** both Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) pages returned HTTP 403 (the mastery learning Toolkit page and the digital technology guidance report). The Evidence for Learning mirror returned 404. EEF figures below come from search snippets and third-party summaries only.
- **Not searched at all:** Central Square Foundation EdTech Tulna, and Evidence for ESSA beyond one Zearn entry.

## 1. Adaptive practice and mastery learning: what do good trials show for primary maths, and what dose and conditions were needed?

### Takeaway
Well-run trials of maths practice software show small positive effects, typically 0.05 to 0.20 SD, and the big outlier (Mindspark, 0.2 to 0.6 SD) came where ordinary teaching was very weak and attendance was supervised. The gains depend on dose, and in normal use only a small minority of pupils reach the recommended dose.

### Cited Findings

**Mindspark (India)**
- Delhi, 2019 paper (lottery-based randomised trial, after-school centres). Lottery winners scored 0.36 SD higher in maths and 0.22 SD higher in Hindi after 4.5 months of access. — [RISE blog by the authors](https://riseprogramme.org/blog/delhi_experiment_sparks_success.html); [J-PAL copy of the Education Next article](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/2019.04.22_Muralidharan_Education%20Next.pdf)
- Same trial: the estimate for 90 days of actual attendance is 0.59 SD in maths. Only 58 percent of those offered the free voucher attended. — [World Bank Development Impact blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/impactevaluations/technoskeptics-pay-heed-computer-assisted-learning-program-delivers-learning-results); [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/?p=49709672)
- Same trial: absolute gains were similar for all pupils. Relative gains were far larger for weaker pupils, because weaker pupils in the control group learned close to nothing in school. — [RISE blog](https://riseprogramme.org/blog/delhi_experiment_sparks_success.html)
- The World Bank reviewers called 0.59 SD unusually large, "25 percent larger than the largest effect" in an earlier review, and treated that as a reason for caution. — [World Bank Development Impact blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/impactevaluations/technoskeptics-pay-heed-computer-assisted-learning-program-delivers-learning-results)
- Rajasthan, 2025 working paper (cluster randomised trial, 80 government "Adarsh" schools, about 6,500 pupils, primary and middle grades, 2017 to 2021). After 18 months: +0.22 SD in maths and +0.20 SD in Hindi. Effects were similar in primary and middle grades. — [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/scaling-personalized-adaptive-learning-pal-math-and-language-india)
- Rajasthan conditions: Mindspark replaced about 25 percent of weekly maths and Hindi lesson time in primary schools. Each school had a lab and a paid lab assistant. Teachers went to the lab with the pupils to answer questions and manage the class. — [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/scaling-personalized-adaptive-learning-pal-math-and-language-india)
- Rajasthan caveats: there was **no effect on school exam results**. When lab-assistant staffing was cut in year three, usage fell. Usage tracked learning gains. — [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/scaling-personalized-adaptive-learning-pal-math-and-language-india)

**US platforms**
- Zearn Math, 2025 working paper (RAND-designed randomised trial, 64 schools in an urban Texas district, grades 3 to 5, two years). The main state-test result was positive but not statistically significant (+0.07 SD overall, +0.10 for pupils not proficient at the start). An exploratory second test (MAP) showed 0.11 SD overall and 0.13 for non-proficient pupils. The authors say the study "did not yield confirmatory evidence". — [Evidence for ESSA](https://evidenceforessa.org/?p=1310); [EdWorkingPaper 25-1211](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai25-1211)
- [Conflict] Zearn's own research page describes the same trial as showing positive, statistically significant impacts. The independent authors frame it as promising but not confirmed. — [Zearn research library](https://about.zearn.org/research-library); [EdWorkingPaper 25-1211](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai25-1211)
- ST Math, 2014 (randomised trial, 52 low-performing schools, grades 3 to 5, independent university authors). The effect on maths scores was "negligible": about 0.05 to 0.10 SD, with no separate estimate statistically significant. — [Rutherford et al. 2014, eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8p87p4cf/qt8p87p4cf_noSplash_17f409dd3fc1e22bd6971dab3d812307.pdf)
- [Conflict] A 2021 report from the developer's own institute re-analysed the same ST Math trial (16,307 pupils) and reported a treatment effect across grades of about 0.06 to 0.10. — [MIND Research Institute 2021](https://info.mindresearch.org/hubfs/pdfs/research-results/ST-Math_RCT_2021_MR4.pdf)
- A widely quoted ST Math effect of 0.36 comes from a **non-randomised** comparison of schools that used the program consistently. It is not trial evidence. — [EdTech Roundup report of the WestEd study](https://www.edtechroundup.org/editorials--press/wested-and-mind-research-institute-release-unprecedented-national-study-on-edtech-math-program)
- DreamBox, 2023 (randomised trial, 115 kindergarten and Grade 1 pupils, Florida). Effects of g = 0.32 on numeracy and 0.14 on geometry. The author warns the true effect may be smaller. — [Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness](https://www.informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/19345747.2023.2174919)
- Vendor or vendor-linked studies report 0.26 SD (Khan Academy, 2022, 99 districts), 0.20 SD (DreamBox, Harvard study), 0.22 SD (i-Ready) and 0.14 SD (IXL). All of these are for pupils who used the program **as recommended**. — [Education Next, "The 5 Percent Problem", 2024](https://www.educationnext.org/5-percent-problem-online-mathematics-programs-may-benefit-most-kids-who-need-it-least/)
- Khan Academy's recommended dose is 30 minutes a week. Only 4.7 percent of pupils reached it. Pupils who reach the dose tend to be high achievers already. — [Education Next, "The 5 Percent Problem", 2024](https://www.educationnext.org/5-percent-problem-online-mathematics-programs-may-benefit-most-kids-who-need-it-least/)
- ASSISTments (older students: Grade 7 homework). A replication trial in North Carolina (2018 to 2020) found a lasting effect of g = 0.10 one year after the program ended. A separate 2020 study reported g = 0.22. — [WestEd long-term effects brief, 2023](https://wested2024.s3.us-west-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/11173937/ASSISTments-Long-Term-Effects-_07-11-23_FINAL-ADA.pdf)
- Khan Academy with Khanmigo (older students: middle school, 18 schools, two-year cluster randomised trial, 2024 to 2026). Practice was built into compulsory daily remedial sessions of 25 to 40 minutes with a teacher present. Result: about 0.06 SD per school year, 0.08 in year two, and 0.14 implied for a full year of active participation. Pupils did about 30 extra minutes of practice a week. — [Oreopoulos and Low 2026, EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)

**Reviews**
- Randomised trials of maths practice software "generally find positive effects in the range of 0.05 to 0.20 standard deviations", with larger effects where adaptive software stands in for especially weak teaching. — [Oreopoulos and Low 2026, literature review section](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)
- Cheung and Slavin (2013 meta-analysis, K-12 maths): average effect d = 0.16 across 74 studies. For randomised studies the effect was smaller, about 0.08. Their summary: "It is a help, but not a breakthrough." — [Best Evidence Encyclopedia](https://bestevidence.org/2021/02/07/mathematics-effectiveness-of-technology/)
- Escueta, Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan (2020 review of rigorous studies, Journal of Economic Literature): software that builds specific skills is "particularly promising", especially in maths. It worked best as an in-class tool or as compulsory homework support. Handing out devices alone generally did not improve learning. — [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/node/4552); [journal record](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20191507)
- EEF Toolkit, mastery learning (the teaching approach, not software): about +5 months of extra progress a year on average. Effects are higher in maths and science (+6 months) than in reading (+3 months). — [EEF mastery learning page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/mastery-learning) (search snippet only; page returned 403)

### Inferences
- The honest range for a platform's practice engine in an ordinary, reasonably taught class is about 0.05 to 0.15 SD a year. Claims above 0.2 SD usually rest on "pupils who used it as recommended", which selects motivated pupils.
- Every successful trial had three things: scheduled time, an adult in the room, and a rule that made use compulsory. None relied on children choosing to log in at home.
- Mindspark's large effects are best read as "adaptive practice beats very weak teaching". They are a poor guide to what software adds in a well-taught international school class.
- The Rajasthan "no effect on school exams" result is a warning. Software that teaches at the child's level may not move grade-level test scores.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm the grades in the Delhi Mindspark trial from a page I opened. My recollection is that pupils were mostly in grades 6 to 9 and that sessions were 90 minutes (half software, half small-group teaching), six days a week. Treat this as unverified.
- The original ASSISTments Maine effect size (Roschelle et al. 2016) was not retrieved.
- The DreamBox Harvard study was not opened. I could not check its grades or design.
- EEF's evidence-strength rating and its primary versus secondary breakdown for mastery learning could not be read (403).
- No trial found on adaptive practice in an international-school or high-income Asian setting.

## 2. Game-based learning in primary maths: what do meta-analyses find, does story help or distract, and does it matter whether the maths is the mechanic?

### Takeaway
Games beat non-game teaching by a small to moderate margin overall (about 0.3 SD across subjects, closer to 0.13 SD for maths), with very mixed results between studies. Story does not reliably help learning and can hurt when it is rich but beside the point; the best single design finding is that the maths should be the game's core mechanic.

### Cited Findings

**Overall effects**
- Clark, Tanner-Smith and Killingsworth (2016 meta-analysis, Review of Educational Research, all subjects, ages 6 to 25, mean sample age 13.4). Games versus non-game teaching: g = 0.33 (95% CI 0.19 to 0.48, 57 studies). Enhanced game designs versus standard versions: g = 0.34. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- Tokac, Novak and Thompson (2019 meta-analysis, maths only, PreK to Grade 12, 24 studies). Small effect of d = 0.13 (p = .02) versus traditional teaching. Effects varied in both size and direction. — [Journal of Computer Assisted Learning record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/jcal.12347)
- Öztop (2022 meta-analysis, primary maths, Turkish studies only, 21 effect sizes). Overall effect of games 0.875. Digital games had a small effect (0.436). **Non-digital games had a large effect (1.032).** — [International Primary Education Research Journal](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/iperj/article/1067794)
- Benavides-Varela and colleagues (2020 meta-analysis, children with maths learning difficulties). Digital interventions helped (mean effect 0.55), but there was "no evidence that videogames offer additional advantages" over digital drill and tutoring. — [Computers & Education, open copy](https://www.research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3345422/2/1-s2.0-S0360131520301512-main.pdf)

**Story and narrative (Clark et al. 2016 moderators)**
- Story relevance: no story g = 0.44; story irrelevant to the learning g = 0.63; story relevant to the learning g = 0.17 (not clearly different from zero). The difference weakened to non-significance after controlling for other game features. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- Story depth: none g = 0.44; thin g = 0.47; medium g = -0.03; thick g = 0.36 (only five studies, very wide interval). Games with no story or a thin story did significantly better than medium-depth stories. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- More "contextualisation" (story plus visual richness combined) went with smaller learning effects (b = -0.07, p = .01). — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- Visual style: schematic g = 0.48; cartoon g = 0.32; realistic g = -0.01. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- "Seductive details" (interesting but irrelevant additions to learning material): a 2020 meta-analysis found learners did worse with them than without. — [Washington State University summary of Sundararajan and Adesope 2020](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning)
- A 2026 conference meta-analysis (50 studies) puts the seductive-details effect at about g = -0.16: real but small. — [AERA 2026 paper summary](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera26/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/2273554/) (secondary; abstract only)

**Other design conditions (Clark et al. 2016)**
- One play session: g = 0.08 (not significant). Multiple sessions: g = 0.44. Total minutes played did not predict the effect. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- Feedback and support: success/fail/points only g = 0.26; enhanced in-game scaffolding g = 0.48; **teacher-provided scaffolding g = 0.58**. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- Play structure: single player without competition g = 0.45; single-player competitive g = -0.06; collaborative team competition g = 0.22 (three studies); multiplayer g = -0.05. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)

**Maths as the mechanic ("intrinsic integration") versus "chocolate-covered broccoli"**
- Habgood and Ainsworth (2011, two experiments, Journal of the Learning Sciences, UK primary-age children, the game "Zombie Division"). Study 1 (58 children): children learned more from the version where division was the core game mechanic than from a version with the same game plus a separate maths quiz, in equal time. Study 2 (16 children): given free choice, children spent about 7 times longer on the integrated version. — [Sheffield Hallam repository copy](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556); [Nottingham repository record](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577510/motivating-children-to-learn-effectively-exploring-the-value-of-intrinsic-integration-in-educational-games)
- Brom and colleagues (2022, grammar game with children, not maths). Intrinsic integration gave longer time on task and better results on some measures. The authors still caution that such games "are not always better than comparable non-game interventions" and that even a minimal game "may be distractive". — [Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.856623/pdf)

### Inferences
- The evidence does not say "story is bad". It says story has a cost in attention and has not been shown to add learning on its own. A thin story that serves the task is the safest reading.
- Clark et al.'s odd result (irrelevant story beat relevant story) did not survive controls. It should not be used to argue for bolted-on stories.
- The meta-analyses test games as a way to teach content. They mostly do not measure whether story makes children want to come back over months. That is a separate claim, and it is weakly evidenced here (one free-choice study with 16 children).
- The Turkish finding that non-digital games beat digital ones is single-country and should be checked, but it points the same way as the manipulatives evidence in section 5.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis found that isolates narrative effects for ages 6 to 10 in maths. Clark et al.'s samples average age 13.
- The Habgood and Ainsworth samples are very small (58 and 16). I found no large replication in maths.
- No evidence found on text-based or MUD-style explorable worlds for primary maths.
- No evidence found on "decisions with consequences" as a design feature for learning at this age.
- Byun and Joung (2018) and Wouters et al. (2013) were named in searches but not retrieved.

## 3. Extrinsic rewards: points, badges, streaks, leaderboards, collectible cards

### Takeaway
Gamification shows small positive average effects in short studies, mostly with older students, and the evidence on young children is thin. Expected tangible rewards for doing or finishing a task tend to reduce later free-choice interest, more so in children than adults, and leaderboards carry a known risk for low performers.

### Cited Findings
- Sailer and Homner (2020 meta-analysis, Educational Psychology Review, 38 publications, mostly older students). Small positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioural outcomes. Effects on motivation and behaviour were less stable than cognitive effects. — [University of Augsburg record](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)
- Same meta-analysis, as reported by a secondary summary: cognitive g about 0.49; behavioural g about 0.25. Game fiction (a story frame) and combining competition with collaboration helped behavioural outcomes. The authors flagged short study periods and possible novelty effects. — [Rework summary](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/edtech-growth/gamification-in-learning) (secondary; not checked against the paper)
- Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999 meta-analysis of 128 experiments). Rewards given for engaging in a task, completing a task, or performing well reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36 and -0.28). Tangible rewards were **more harmful for children than for college students**. Unexpected rewards, and rewards not tied to the task, did not undermine motivation. — [Psychological Bulletin 1999, full text copy](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); [2001 follow-up in Review of Educational Research](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- [Conflict] These findings were disputed at the time. A published comment argued the meta-analysis was misused, and other reviewers argue tangible rewards can be given in ways that have neutral or positive effects. — [Comment on Deci, Koestner and Ryan, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589298)
- Hanus and Fox (2015, one university course, about 80 students, 16 weeks; older students). The class with badges and a leaderboard ended with lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment, and lower final exam scores, than the plain class. — [Computers & Education, via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265644737_Assessing_the_effects_of_gamification_in_the_classroom_A_longitudinal_study_on_intrinsic_motivation_social_comparison_satisfaction_effort_and_academic_performance)
- [Conflict] A 2022 study in online physics classes (older students) found badges and leaderboards, alone or together, did not harm quiz scores or motivation. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8916940)
- A 2024 field experiment with elementary pupils compared badges and leaderboards. Achievement-type motivation rose. The effect on intrinsic motivation was "limited, except for the enhancement of interest". — [Mohammed, Fatemah and Hassan 2024, Simulation & Gaming](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10468781241237389)
- A review of leaderboards in education reports that effects differ by learner and that poorly designed leaderboards can demotivate lower-ranked users. — [Singapore Management University review](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/sis_research/article/11046/viewcontent/Effects_of_the_Use_of_Leaderboards_in_Education_pv.pdf)
- In games research, single-player competitive play showed no learning benefit (g = -0.06), against g = 0.45 for non-competitive single play. — [Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- Counter-evidence worth knowing: in the same meta-analysis, game versions that added only points or badges did no worse (g = 0.53, 17 studies) than versions with more elaborate mechanics (g = 0.25, 40 studies). The difference was not significant. But games that gave only success/fail/points feedback (g = 0.26) did worse than those with real scaffolding (0.48 to 0.58). — [Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)

### Inferences
- Rewards are not all one thing. The evidence separates (a) rewards that tell a child they got better at something from (b) rewards that pay a child for showing up. The second kind is where the harm findings sit. Streaks are the clearest case of the second kind.
- A collectible earned by completing a real task is "completion-contingent" in Deci's terms (d = -0.36 on later free choice). Whether a card that records an achievement behaves like a payment or like feedback is not settled by this evidence. What would prove a card system harmless: children still choosing the tasks when cards are withdrawn.
- Points and badges can raise short-term activity without harming test scores. The open risk is what happens after months, which almost no study measures.
- A public leaderboard in one class of 8-year-olds is the riskiest of these features, because every child knows who is last.

### Gaps
- I found no large randomised trial of points, badges, streaks or leaderboards for learning in children aged 6 to 10. The elementary studies found are small and mostly from single schools.
- I found **no research at all on streaks** or on **collectible cards** as learning rewards.
- I found no long-term study (a full school year or more) of gamification in primary maths.
- Sailer and Homner's exact figures were not checked against the paper.

## 4. Video lessons and live online classes for children under 10: attention, learning, and the need for an adult

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on recorded or live online maths lessons for 6 to 10 year olds is thin. What exists says young children learn less from screens than from a person, that primary pupils made little or no progress when taught remotely at home, and that online-only instruction generally trails in-person teaching.

### Cited Findings
- "Video deficit" (meta-analysis, ages 0 to 6): young children often learn less from video than from the same content face to face, by about half a standard deviation in some domains. Younger than the target age. — [Strouse and Samson, meta-analysis of the video deficit](https://red.library.usd.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1002&context=se-fp)
- Engzell, Frey and Verhagen (2021, PNAS, national test data on about 350,000 Dutch primary pupils). After an 8-week closure with online learning, in a country with strong broadband and short closures, pupils lost about 3 percentile points or 0.08 SD. This is about one-fifth of a school year, the same length as the closure. Pupils "made little or no progress while learning from home". Losses were up to 60 percent larger for children from less-educated homes. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8092566); [University of Oxford summary](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2021-04-08-children-learned-little-or-nothing-during-school-closures-despite-online-learning)
- Betthäuser, Bach-Mortensen and Engzell (2023 meta-analysis, Nature Human Behaviour, 42 studies, 15 countries, all school ages). Pupils lost about a third of a school year. Losses were larger in maths than in reading; the authors suggest parents are better able to help with reading. The review could not separate the effect of different remote-teaching methods. — [UCL copy of the paper](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10164709/1/A_systematic_review_and_meta_analysis_of_the_evidence_on_learning_during_the_COVID_19_pandemic.pdf); [Phys.org report](https://phys.org/news/2023-01-schoolkids-lost-year-pandemic.html)
- Escueta et al. (2020 review, mostly secondary and university students): students taught in person generally outperformed those in online-only classes. Blended courses did about as well as in-person courses. Evidence on online courses was still "nascent". — [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/node/4552)
- Live video with young children: a study of synchronous online book reading found young children can learn from interactive online read-alouds, but on-task attention needs support and distractions need to be reduced. — [Computers & Education 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131522001993)
- Recorded video can help older primary pupils who read weakly. A 2025 experiment with Grade 5 and 6 pupils found videos gave better delayed retention and lower mental load than illustrated text. The benefit was larger for weaker readers. Older than the target age. — [Learning and Instruction 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225001240)
- UNESCO's 2023 global report states that online connections are no substitute for human interaction. — [UNESCO GEM 2023 summary](https://www.unesco.at/fileadmin/Redaktion/Bildung/2023GE-Report_TechEd_Summary.pdf)

### Inferences
- The pandemic studies are not clean tests of video lessons. They mix video, worksheets, parent help and disruption. But they are the largest real-world test of "children under 11 learn maths through a screen at home", and the result was close to no progress.
- The maths-worse-than-reading pattern suggests maths at this age depends more on a knowledgeable adult responding to errors than reading does.
- Video has one real, evidenced advantage for this project's age group: it lowers the reading load for weak readers (see section 8).

### Gaps
- I found no randomised trial of recorded video maths lessons versus teacher-led lessons for ages 6 to 10 in normal (non-pandemic) conditions.
- I found no good evidence on live online classes (one teacher, many children) for under-10s in maths: not on attention span, not on class size, not on learning.
- I found no sourced figure for how long an 8-year-old attends to a video lesson. Popular "minutes per year of age" rules were not found in any research source and should not be cited.
- The age range of the Dutch sample was not confirmed from a page I opened. Sources say "primary school pupils".

## 5. Concrete materials, "concrete, pictorial, abstract", and understanding before procedure

### Takeaway
This is the best-supported area in these notes. Concrete materials give small to moderate gains over symbols-only teaching when they are used with clear guidance and linked to the idea, and US and UK evidence bodies both recommend them for this age group with strong ratings.

### Cited Findings
- Carbonneau, Marley and Selig (2013 meta-analysis, Journal of Educational Psychology, kindergarten to college, N = 7,237). Small to moderate effects in favour of teaching with manipulatives compared with abstract symbols only. The size of the effect depended on how they were taught with. — [Arizona State University record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-efficacy-of-teaching-mathematics-with-conc/)
- Same meta-analysis, per a secondary summary: mean effect about 0.37. The amount of instructional guidance, the topic (fractions highest at 0.69), the age of learners and the teaching time all changed the result. — [Typeset summary](https://typeset.io/papers/a-meta-analysis-of-the-efficacy-of-teaching-mathematics-with-4pur9pc11i) (secondary; not checked against the paper)
- What Works Clearinghouse practice guide (2021, grades K to 6, pupils struggling with maths). Six recommendations, **all rated "strong" evidence**. Recommendation 3: "Use a well-chosen set of concrete and semi-concrete representations to support students' learning of mathematical concepts and procedures." Recommendation 1 is systematic instruction. Recommendation 4 is the number line. Recommendation 6 is regular timed activities to build fluency. — [WWC practice guide page](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/26)
- EEF guidance for ages 7 to 14 (2017, updated 2022), Recommendation 2: use manipulatives and representations. They "are just tools"; how they are used matters. They should be used purposefully, linked to the underlying idea, and removed once the pupil is independent. — [EEF Improving Mathematics in Key Stages 2 and 3](https://d10a08pz293654.cloudfront.net/production/documents/EEF-Improving-Mathematics-in-Key-Stages-2-and-3-2022-Update_2025-05-13-083225_lvps.pdf)
- "Concreteness fading" (start concrete, move to pictures, end with symbols). A 2014 systematic review found "some direct empirical support" for the full three-step sequence. — [Fyfe, McNeil, Son and Goldstone 2014](https://www.academia.edu/14450861/Concreteness_Fading_in_Mathematics_and_Science_Instruction_a_Systematic_Review)
- In experiments with children, the fading sequence gave better transfer than concrete-only, abstract-only, or the reverse order. — [McNeil and Fyfe 2012, Learning and Instruction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475212000333); [Fyfe, McNeil and Borjas 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475214000942)
- Caveat: the support comes mainly from basic maths concepts, and results in physics are mixed. — [Review article, German National Library copy](https://d-nb.info/1223824624/34)
- Concept before procedure: the relationship runs both ways. There is broad agreement that understanding supports procedural skill. Whether practising procedures builds understanding is more contested, but the 2015 review argues it does. — [Rittle-Johnson, Schneider and Star 2015, Educational Psychology Review](https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/574613/mod_resource/content/2/s10648-015-9302-x.pdf)
- Öztop (2022, Turkey, primary maths): non-digital games had a much larger effect (1.032) than digital games (0.436). — [International Primary Education Research Journal](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/iperj/article/1067794)

### Inferences
- The evidence backs "see it and handle it, then fade to symbols". It does not back "concept first, always". Both evidence bodies also recommend explicit teaching and fluency practice. A design with hands-on tasks and no fluency practice would be only half-supported.
- Hands-on tasks help only when an adult or the task itself makes the link to the maths explicit. A real-life task that is fun but not tied back to the number idea is the manipulatives version of "seductive details".
- Screen-only practice cannot deliver the physical step. Virtual manipulatives may partly stand in, but I did not find evidence on that here.

### Gaps
- No evidence retrieved on virtual (on-screen) manipulatives versus physical ones at this age.
- The WWC guide is about pupils who struggle. Its strong ratings may not transfer in full to a whole class.
- No evidence found on real-life hands-on tasks set by a digital adventure and done away from the screen.
- Carbonneau's detailed numbers are secondary. The EEF "concrete, pictorial, abstract" wording itself was not found in a page I opened.

## 6. AI tutors and chatbots with children (2023 to 2026): learning effects, over-reliance, and safety for under-13s

### Takeaway
There is almost no rigorous evidence on generative AI tutors for children under 11; the good trials are with teenagers. Those trials show that an AI which gives answers raises practice scores but lowers later unaided scores, that guardrails remove the harm without yet showing a gain, and that pupils rarely use an AI tutor for real dialogue even when it is free and built into lessons.

### Cited Findings
- Bastani and colleagues (2025, PNAS; randomised field experiment, about 1,000 pupils in grades 9 to 11, one high school in Turkey, autumn 2023; older students). A plain ChatGPT-style tool ("GPT Base") raised practice scores by 48 percent. A tutor with teacher-written hints and no direct answers ("GPT Tutor") raised them by 127 percent. On the later exam without AI, the GPT Base group scored **17 percent lower than the control group**. The GPT Tutor group scored about the same as control. — [SSRN paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486); [PNAS record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2422633122); [Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education)
- Same study: pupils used the plain tool as a "crutch". Caveats: one school, one subject, and chatbots were still new to pupils. — [Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education)
- Oreopoulos and Low (2026 working paper; two-year cluster randomised trial, 18 middle schools in Tennessee; older students). Khanmigo was set to coach, not give answers. Effect: 0.06 to 0.08 SD per school year. "These gains resemble those from Khan Academy practice without AI assistance." — [EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)
- Same study, usage: 96 percent of pupils messaged the AI at least once. The median pupil sent no message on two-thirds of practice days and used it in only 17 percent of the sessions where they made a mistake. Only 14.5 percent of messages contained a maths question or a step of reasoning. The authors conclude the limit is engagement, not access. — [EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)
- Same paper, on the field: experimental evidence on AI tutoring "remains small, short-horizon, and concentrated outside ordinary public school instruction". — [EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)
- Google LearnLM with Eedi (2025 preprint; exploratory randomised trial, 165 pupils, five UK secondary schools; older students; authors include the developers). Human tutors checked every AI message before it was sent and approved 76.4 percent with little or no change. Pupils did at least as well as with human tutors alone (66.2 versus 60.7 percent success on new problems). The safety claim rests on the human check. — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633)
- Tutor CoPilot (2024 preprint; randomised trial, 900 tutors and 1,800 K-12 pupils in Title I schools, maths). Here the AI advises the **human tutor**, not the child. Pupils were 4 percentage points more likely to master the topic (62 to 66 percent), and 9 points more with lower-rated tutors. Tutors asked more guiding questions and gave fewer answers. — [arXiv 2410.03017](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03017v1); [National Student Support Accelerator summary](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise)
- [Conflict] The primary paper describes the Tutor CoPilot sample as K-12. A Stanford summary describes about 1,000 elementary pupils. I could not resolve this. — [arXiv 2410.03017](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03017v1); [NSSA summary](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise)
- World Bank, Nigeria (2024 pilot, randomised; about 800 first-year senior secondary pupils; English, not maths; older students). Six weeks of after-school sessions, twice a week, with a general chatbot **guided by teachers**: about 0.3 SD. Each extra session attended added to the gain. — [World Bank blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria)
- University level, for context only: a Harvard physics trial is titled "AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning", using a carefully designed tutor. — [Scientific Reports 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6)

**Safety for under-13s**
- UNICEF Guidance on AI and Children 3.0 (2025): all under-18s keep their child rights. Reaching the "age of digital consent" (13 in many countries) does not mean a child should be treated as an adult. It notes civil-society calls to ban companion chatbots for under-18s. — [UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/media/11991/file/UNICEF-Innocenti-Guidance-on-AI-and-Children-3-2025.pdf)
- Common Sense Media with Stanford (April 2025 risk assessment): social AI companion chatbots are rated "unacceptable" for anyone under 18. Risks named include compulsive emotional attachment. — [Common Sense Media risk assessment](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/pug/csm-ai-risk-assessment-social-ai-companions_final.pdf); [The Markup report](https://themarkup.org/artificial-intelligence/2025/04/30/kids-should-avoid-ai-companion-bots-under-force-of-law-assessment-says)
- Common Sense Media (January 2026): 27 percent of AI toy outputs tested were inappropriate for children. AI toys were rated "unacceptable". The report notes children cannot give meaningful consent. — [Common Sense Media AI toys assessment](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/ai-ratings/csm-ai-risk-assessment-ai-toys-01142026.pdf)
- UNICEF (2026 policy brief on AI companions) calls for stricter safeguards on chatbots likely to be reached by children. — [UNICEF policy brief](https://www.unicef.org/media/181131/file/UNICEF-When-AI-becomes-friend-policy-brief-2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- The one robust learning finding is about design: an AI that hands over answers harms learning; an AI that is constrained to hints does not. Constraint is what the evidence supports, not the AI's presence.
- No trial yet shows a constrained AI tutor beating plain practice software in a school. The Khanmigo result says the AI layer added nothing measurable, mainly because pupils did not talk to it.
- The designs with positive results all kept a human in the loop: tutors approving messages, teachers guiding sessions, or AI advising the adult. None left children alone with a chatbot.
- All of this is evidence from teenagers. An 8-year-old types slowly, reads less, and is more likely to treat a chatbot as a person. Applying these results to Grade 3 is extrapolation in both directions: benefits and harms are both unmeasured.
- The safety assessments are about companion chatbots and toys, not narrow maths tutors. They still show that open-ended AI conversation with young children produces inappropriate output at a rate that needs an adult check.
- A point to test that could prove a claim wrong: no study here examines an AI used as a narrator or world-runner (where the code checks the answers) and not as a tutor. That use is simply unstudied. It is not shown safe or effective, and it is not shown harmful.

### Gaps
- **No rigorous randomised trial of a generative AI tutor for children aged 6 to 10 was found.**
- No study found on over-reliance in primary-age children.
- I did not retrieve legal sources on the under-13 rules (US COPPA, or the age limits in AI providers' terms of service). These need a separate check.
- No peer-reviewed study on young children's trust in, or attachment to, chatbots was opened. One systematic review preprint was listed but not read.
- A WestEd Khanmigo trial (47 schools) was mentioned only by a low-quality blog. I could not verify it and left its numbers out.
- Several of the studies above are preprints or working papers, not yet peer reviewed, and two have developer authors.

## 7. The teacher's role: supplement or replacement, and what the evidence says about blended use

### Takeaway
The evidence consistently favours technology that a teacher runs as a supplement inside scheduled time over technology that replaces teaching. Good, impartial evidence is scarce, and much of what exists is funded by the companies selling the products.

### Cited Findings
- UNESCO Global Education Monitoring Report (2023): "Good, impartial evidence on the impact of education technology is in short supply." "Some education technology can improve some types of learning in some contexts." When evidence comes only from the companies, it may be biased. — [UNESCO GEM 2023 summary](https://www.unesco.at/fileadmin/Redaktion/Bildung/2023GE-Report_TechEd_Summary.pdf); [Teacher magazine summary](https://www.teachermagazine.com/in_en/articles/unesco-calls-for-smartphone-ban-in-schools)
- Same report: giving pupils computers does not improve learning if teachers are not involved. Technology should "support, but never supplant" human interaction in teaching. — [UNESCO GEM 2023 summary](https://www.unesco.at/fileadmin/Redaktion/Bildung/2023GE-Report_TechEd_Summary.pdf)
- A 2024 update of the Cheung and Slavin maths technology review found an overall effect of +0.16, with **supplemental** computer-assisted instruction showing the largest effect (+0.19). — [Best Evidence Encyclopedia](https://bestevidence.org/2021/02/07/mathematics-effectiveness-of-technology/) (secondary)
- Escueta et al. (2020): maths software worked best as an in-class tool or compulsory homework support. Online-only classes generally did worse than in-person classes. Blended did about the same as in-person. — [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/node/4552)
- EEF guidance report "Using Digital Technology to Improve Learning": schools should decide whether a technology will supplement, enhance or replace existing teaching. — [EEF guidance report](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/digital) (page returned 403); [Research School summary](https://researchschool.org.uk/bradford/news/3-takeaways-from-the-digital-technology-guidance-report)
- In games, teacher-provided scaffolding gave the largest effect of any support type (g = 0.58, against 0.26 for points-only feedback). — [Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- Mindspark in Rajasthan needed teachers in the lab and a paid lab assistant. Usage dropped when the assistant role was cut. — [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/scaling-personalized-adaptive-learning-pal-math-and-language-india)
- Khanmigo's trial effect came with compulsory, teacher-supervised daily sessions. — [EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)
- Voluntary use reaches few: 4.7 percent of pupils met Khan Academy's recommended weekly dose. — [Education Next, 2024](https://www.educationnext.org/5-percent-problem-online-mathematics-programs-may-benefit-most-kids-who-need-it-least/)
- Human tutoring remains the benchmark: randomised trials put the average effect of tutoring programs at about 0.3 SD, against 0.05 to 0.20 for practice software. — [Oreopoulos and Low 2026, literature review](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Replacement" has one evidenced success story: Mindspark replacing part of very weak instruction, with adults still present. There is no evidence here of software replacing a competent teacher and doing as well.
- Much of the measured benefit of edtech may come from the structure around it (protected time, an adult, a routine) and not from the software's features. The Khanmigo study says this almost directly.
- A tool used by one teacher with one class has the conditions the trials found necessary. It lacks what a large platform has: tested content at scale and independent evaluation. Neither kind of product gets its effectiveness from this evidence for free.

### Gaps
- The EEF digital guidance report's four recommendations could not be read directly (403). Only one third-party summary was seen.
- The full UNESCO report was not opened. Quotes come from the official summary PDF as surfaced by search, and from news coverage.
- I found no head-to-head trial of "teacher-run supplement" versus "home use of the same software" in primary maths.
- Major, Francis and Tsapali (2021) on personalised technology was searched for but not found.

## 8. Reading load: text-heavy maths tasks for 8-year-olds and for children learning in a second language

### Takeaway
Language difficulty in a maths task lowers maths scores, most of all for second-language learners and weaker pupils, and simplifying the wording helps. Direct evidence for Grade 3 is thin and mostly correlational, but US guidance rates explicit teaching of maths language and word problems as strongly evidenced for grades K to 6.

### Cited Findings
- Abedi and Lord (2001 experiment, 1,174 Grade 8 pupils; older students). Rewriting test items in simpler language, with the same maths, raised scores slightly. English learners, pupils in lower maths classes and pupils from poorer homes gained most. — [The Language Factor in Mathematics Tests](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-language-factor-in-mathematics-tests-1o16uxplej.pdf)
- Earlier national US test analysis: the gap for English learners was larger on longer and more language-heavy maths questions. — [CRESST report on language background in NAEP mathematics](https://cresst.org/publication/final-report-of-language-background-as-a-variable-in-naep-mathematics-performance/)
- Martiniello (2009): non-mathematical language complexity made word problems unfairly harder for English learners than for other pupils of the same maths ability. — [Martiniello, copy hosted at UC Santa Cruz](https://people.ucsc.edu/~ktellez/martiniello.pdf)
- Clinton, Basaraba and Walkington (2018 systematic review): the effect of language features on word-problem performance differs between English learners and others, and changes with grade level. — [University of North Dakota repository](https://commons.und.edu/efr-fac/6)
- UK doctoral study of children with English as an additional language: weaker language comprehension and trouble turning arithmetic into word problems, with decoding and plain arithmetic equal to their peers. — [White Rose thesis](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/32044/1/Final%20Thesis.pdf) (thesis, not peer-reviewed journal)
- Orosco and Reed (2024, Grade 3 English learners with maths difficulties): a comprehension-strategy intervention for word problems was tested and reported as beneficial. — [National Student Support Accelerator summary](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/supplemental-intervention-third-grade-english-learners-significant-problem-solving) (results not read in detail)
- Wang, Lopez-Reyna and Kim (2023 review of word-problem interventions for elementary English learners at risk in maths): more than half the studies worked on text comprehension. — [ERIC record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1399914)
- WWC practice guide (2021, grades K to 6), both rated **strong**: Recommendation 2, "Teach clear and concise mathematical language and support students' use of the language". Recommendation 5, "Provide deliberate instruction on word problems". — [WWC practice guide page](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/26)
- For Grade 5 and 6 pupils, video gave better delayed retention and lower mental load than illustrated text, with a larger benefit for weaker readers. Decoding and reading comprehension predicted how much pupils learned. Older than the target age. — [Learning and Instruction 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225001240)
- Seductive details (interesting but irrelevant text) cause a small loss in learning, about g = -0.16 in a 2026 analysis, linked to added mental load. — [AERA 2026 paper summary](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera26/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/2273554/) (secondary)

### Inferences
- A typed, text-heavy maths task tests reading and maths together. For a second-language learner the reading can be the harder half. A wrong answer then says little about the maths.
- The evidence supports two different responses, and they pull in different directions: cut the language load so the maths shows (Abedi), and teach maths language and word problems on purpose (WWC). A design can do both only if it controls which one a given task is doing.
- Story text that is not needed to solve the problem is a measured cost, small on average, and likely larger for children who read slowly in a second language. That last step is my inference; no study here tests it for this group.
- Options the evidence points to for lowering load without removing story: shorter sentences, audio or read-aloud, pictures, and adjustable reading levels. Only the general "video/audio helps weak readers" finding is sourced here, and it is from ages 10 to 12.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis on reading load in maths tasks specifically for Grade 3 or for 8-year-old second-language learners. The key experiment (Abedi and Lord) is Grade 8.
- A 2022 best-evidence review of reading interventions for English learners found no studies that included Grade 3 English learners at all, which shows how thin this age band is. — [Roberts et al. 2022, mirror copy](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-021-09629-2.pdf)
- No evidence found on typing load (as opposed to reading load) for 8-year-olds on tablets.
- No evidence found on adjustable reading levels or text-to-speech inside maths games for this age.
- Flores and Urrutia (2022), a meta-analysis on language and word problems, was listed in search but its results were not retrieved.
- I did not confirm the grade level in Martiniello's study from the page.
